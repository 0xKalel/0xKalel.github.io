## A business already in operation

StoragePal customers book collection, storage, and return of their belongings online. Staff use the platform to manage orders, stock, invoicing, and payments.

I inherited an existing application and improved it in stages. I set priorities, write specifications, review work, and implement major parts myself. Other developers contributed reporting, CRM integration, and reservation features. I also built the [SPS warehouse app](/work/sps/) end to end.

## Start where staff were blocked

Inventory and reservation screens were among my first priorities. Staff needed them every day, but filters could take minutes or time out, and full exports could exhaust server memory.

I moved pagination and filtering into database queries and introduced batched exports. Later, I consolidated **twelve backoffice pages** around shared definitions for columns, filters, sorting, and exports.

This involved limits as well as improvements. Some expensive derived columns are not sortable, and several unfiltered exports still require a filter to finish within the server timeout. The objective was to make daily work manageable while improving an inherited system.

## Cache what customers share

The catalogue rendered too much data and repeated database work on each visit. I first reduced queries and added eager loading and indexes. Later, I introduced a server-rendered page built from cached catalogue data.

The cache holds product information by locale. Customer quantities are merged into each response, and custom items stay outside the shared cache. Backoffice changes trigger invalidation; a 24-hour expiry provides a backstop. Import paths still need consistent invalidation coverage.

This was time to first byte, not full page load. The old code ran against the current database; the measurement is retained in a session note, without a reproducible script.

I also combined a three-page payment flow into one page. That describes pages in the payment flow, rather than a change in analytics funnel events.

## Establish the live claim before chasing payment

Unpaid balances carried into later invoices could still be treated as separate claims. Extending retries before resolving that would risk collecting the wrong amount.

I established one rule for the live claim: the current invoice at the head of the chain. The payment-attempt engine checks that claim and any existing payment intent before charging. I then added retry handling, a review queue for blocked payments, and bounded reminders that check payment state again before sending.

These protections apply to the rebuilt attempt engine. They do not establish that every checkout and refund path has the same safeguards.

## Price transport around the work involved

I replaced coarse regional quotes with an engine that considers driving distance, vehicle capacity, crew, and building access. Customer care and logistics helped shape the parameters. Collections and returns now use the same engine.

Transport revenue was **34% higher** in the twelve months after the collection-pricing launch than in the preceding twelve months. Overall volume and customer mix changed too, so this is an observed business result, not an isolated measure of the engine’s effect.

## Keep AI proposals inside the business rules

The shopping assistant proposes items from text or photos. Server code rejects unknown catalogue items, limits quantities, and supplies the prices. Customers can edit the proposal, and the cart endpoint prices each line again.

The model helps the customer describe what they need. The catalogue and pricing rules determine what can be sold and at what price.
