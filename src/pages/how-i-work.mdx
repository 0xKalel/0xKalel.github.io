## Decide what matters now

I ask what needs to change, who depends on it, and what a useful first result looks like. Then I split what is needed now from what can wait, and cut the work into releases that each deliver a complete, usable workflow.

For RavenClip, that meant [unattended publishing first](/work/ravenclip/#start-with-a-complete-workflow), then reshaping the product around its paying customers. At StoragePal, I started with the operations screens, because staff needed them every day.

## Make the technical choices fit the context

I weigh performance, data correctness, failure recovery, and the cost of maintaining the system. A pattern has to solve a problem we actually have.

At StoragePal, shared catalog data can be cached, but [each customer's cart stays separate](/work/storagepal/#cache-what-customers-share). For SPS, I [removed offline support](/work/sps/#remove-complexity-the-warehouse-does-not-need) once I knew every warehouse had internet. Less code to maintain mattered more than scanning without a connection.

## Use AI with clear responsibility

AI helps me build, investigate, and test scoped changes. Priorities, pricing, architecture, and acceptance stay my responsibility. I write down constraints, challenge proposals, and ask for proof that the result works.

That includes learning from failures. An AI-assisted bulk edit once reached RavenClip production with a syntax error. I added a check that PHP files parse, and a deploy step that stops before database changes if syntax is broken.

[Read the incident and what changed](/blog/2026/09/25/ravenclip-engineering/#building-with-ai-and-owning-the-mistakes).

## Keep the team informed and follow the result

I make decisions, progress, and problems visible. Specs and notes give the team enough context to carry work forward, and I stay involved after release.

Errors, staff feedback, costs, and real use decide what to improve next. When the evidence changes, I change the plan.
